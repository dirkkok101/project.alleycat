# Rebuild the 1984 alley as cute arcade

Alley Cat on the IBM PC is a small game with a clear structure, which makes it a good base for a remake. It has **one alley hub screen, six interior room scenes (five challenges, since the fishbowl takes two scenes), and a love scene** that can only be reached after winning a room. Winning the love scene gives an extra life and raises difficulty by one step, on a ladder of **eight internal levels (0–7)**. Hazards push and knock you around more often than they kill, and the broom is the clearest example. Nearly every exact number available for the PC version (point values, time-bonus formulas, difficulty tables) comes from **one unofficial, partly LLM-written disassembly and fan port**. No manual or review gives any point value, so treat every score in this report as unverified until it has been checked in the archive.org emulator. The original game is **not public domain**: ownership passed from Synapse to Brøderbund and then to successor companies, and IBM published the PC version. The remake should therefore have a new name, new character names, and original art, music and code, and borrow only the mechanics. The research points to this direction: chibi-proportioned pixel art at a low fixed resolution, CGA cyan/magenta used as accent colours plus an optional "1984 mode", a chiptune-hybrid "kawaii pop" score built from layered stems, and **Phaser 4 + Vite + TypeScript** as the default stack. Several of these are real forks in the road, and the closing "Decisions needed" section sets them out as options with a recommendation for each.

## The original is one hub, five challenges and a love scene

On PC, the alley is a single screen. From top to bottom it has three washing lines with rows of windows between them, then a fence, a row of dustbins, and the pavement. A mouse runs along each line and can be caught by leaping into it. **A bulldog appears only when the cat is on the ground**, and touching it costs a life. Windows open from time to time, and people throw objects such as a phone or a shoe out of them. Jumping into an open window starts a room ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game)); [FRGCB](http://frgcb.blogspot.com/2015/04/alley-cat-synapse-software-1983.html)). Most rooms share one more hazard: a broom that is always moving. Footprints the cat leaves along the bottom edge keep it busy. When there are none, it chases the cat. **The broom never kills.** It shoves the cat into other dangers or sweeps it out of the window and back into the alley ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game)); [VGJUNK](http://retrovania-vgjunk.blogspot.com/2018/11/alley-cat-atari-800.html); [Greatest Games](https://greatestgames.substack.com/p/the-classic-pc-gaming-era-1977-1989-e37)).

The brief contains a few names that should be corrected before they reach the design docs. The player cat is **Freddy** and the girlfriend is **Felicia**, not "Cleo" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game)); [Data Driven Gamer](https://datadrivengamer.blogspot.com/2021/07/game-276-alley-cat.html)). There is no separate plant or ivy room: the "plants" are ferns or vases on top of the bookshelf. There is no vacuum cleaner either; the broom is the only household hazard any source mentions. Since the remake will rename everyone anyway, the useful point is to avoid copying even the corrected names.

### A loop that escalates with every kiss

The core loop is simple and fully documented: alley → window → room → win → love scene. If the love scene succeeds, the player gets a life and difficulty goes up by one. If the cat falls, it goes back to the alley and must win another room before trying again. After a room is won, a female cat calls from the alley windows ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game)); [DDG](https://datadrivengamer.blogspot.com/2021/07/game-276-alley-cat.html)). The PC version does not pick rooms at random. It draws from **pools that depend on difficulty**: pool 0 is 4,4,1,1, pool 1 is 3,3,5,5, and pool 2 is 6,6,4,1. A harder pool of all five rooms is used at levels 3 and 7, and no room comes up three times in a row ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). FRGCB independently observed that easy DOS levels offer only a few rooms ([FRGCB](http://frgcb.blogspot.com/2015/04/alley-cat-synapse-software-1983.html)). This is a good design to keep, because it teaches the rooms gradually instead of throwing all five at a new player.

The player chooses a starting skill: Kitten (0), House Cat (1), Tomcat (2) or Alley Cat (3). Each successful love scene adds one level, up to a maximum of 7. Throw rate, enemy spawn and chase chance, dog count and helpings per dish all rise from tables indexed 0–7. For example, `enemy_chase_chance` goes 16, 32, 48 … 128, and helpings per dish go 1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4 ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). Wikipedia instead says difficulty rises until "level 30". That claim is uncited and probably refers to the on-screen level counter or the Atari version ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game))). These tables also come from the unofficial disassembly, so they should be checked like the scoring numbers.

### Momentum jumps and a CPU-paced clock

On PC you control the cat with the cursor keys, and Alt (or the joystick button) does special actions. You start with **3 lives, to a maximum of 9**. There is no visible countdown, but a time bonus starts counting down when you enter a room ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). Jumps depend on momentum. A standing hop goes high but barely forward, while a running jump needs a run-up, and on Atari momentum is lost on landing ([DDG](https://datadrivengamer.blogspot.com/2021/07/game-276-alley-cat.html); [VGJUNK](http://retrovania-vgjunk.blogspot.com/2018/11/alley-cat-atari-800.html)). Freddy speeds up faster on DOS than on Atari, which makes him easier to steer ([FRGCB](http://frgcb.blogspot.com/2015/04/alley-cat-synapse-software-1983.html)). One detail matters when checking feel: the PC game is **effectively CPU-paced for a 4.77 MHz machine**. Its timers use 18.2 Hz BIOS ticks, and when the dog is off screen most alley routines run only every fourth pass of the main loop ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). The emulator's speed setting therefore changes how the game feels. The exact jump arcs, the fishbowl air timer, and what Alt does in each room were not found in any source.

## Every scoring number traces back to one unofficial disassembly

All PC point values come from beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat, a port "rebuilt from the program's disassembly" of `CAT.EXE` ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). Its companion disassembly says it was "100% written by LLM, with a lot of guidance from a human" ([gmegidish/alleycat-disassembly](https://github.com/gmegidish/alleycat-disassembly)). The table names in it were chosen by the disassemblers, not by Synapse. It is the best evidence available, but it is not authoritative. Wikipedia only says completed challenges award points "proportional to the time taken" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game))). The Atari version uses a different system, a "cat-o-meter" of 48 cat heads ([VGJUNK](http://retrovania-vgjunk.blogspot.com/2018/11/alley-cat-atari-800.html)).

For the remake, how much the numbers matter depends on what the game is trying to be. A "modern arcade" game will add combos, multipliers and score pops in any case. The original's overall shape is worth keeping whatever the exact values are: rooms are worth about 1,500–4,000 points, a speed bonus decays to zero within about a minute, and the love scene multiplier grows steeply. Exact values matter only if a faithful "classic mode" is planned.

## Not public domain: borrow the rules, rebuild everything else

Alley Cat was designed and scored by **Bill Williams** (1960–1998), building on a one-screen prototype by John Harris. Synapse released it on Atari 8-bit in 1983, and **IBM published the PC/PCjr version in 1984** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game)); [Wikipedia: Bill Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Williams_(game_designer))). Brøderbund bought Synapse in late 1984 and retired the label in 1985. Wikipedia lists The Learning Company as successor but does not say where the catalogue rights went ([Wikipedia: Synapse Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse_Software)). Nobody found a current rights holder, and IBM may still hold some rights to the PC port. "Abandonware" has no legal standing. Even the fan port's own notice reads "Alley Cat © 1984 Synapse Software / IBM" ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). Fan remakes have stayed online for years without any known takedown: *Alley Cat 2* (2006), Joflof's *Remeow Edition* (2018), the ZX Spectrum *Misifu Remeow*, and the disassembly ports ([Indie Retro News](https://www.indieretronews.com/2018/03/alley-cat-remeow-edition-remake-with.html); [itch.io: Misifu Remeow](https://jbinary.itch.io/misifu-remeow)). That shows tolerance, not permission.

The practical advice, which is a design recommendation and not legal advice, follows from this. Game mechanics such as "jump in a window to start a mini-game" are generally treated as ideas, which copyright does not protect. Sprites, music, code and character names are expression, which it does protect. So the remake should **use the mechanics as inspiration only**. It should not reuse sprites (including ones taken from the vibe-alleycat extractor), the title melody, or code from the disassembly, and it should not use "Freddy" or "Felicia". The working title "Cute Alley Cat" also needs care. "Alley cat" is a common phrase that unrelated games already use (Solar Software's C64 *Alley Cat* and Graftgold's *Alleykat*) ([Greatest Games](https://greatestgames.substack.com/p/the-classic-pc-gaming-era-1977-1989-e37)). But a name built on "Alley Cat" also deliberately signals the 1984 game. A new name with its own identity is lower-risk, and the store-page copy can still mention the inspiration. No trademark search was done, so any name candidate needs one. The music has the same problem. The only sourced music credit is an **original title theme by Williams** ([VGMRips](https://vgmrips.net/packs/pack/alley-cat-ibm-pcjr); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(video_game))), and no source supports the idea that the game used public-domain tunes. Treat the theme as copyrighted. Bent Fabric's 1962 hit "Alley Cat" is a separate commercial song with no known link to the game ([Wikipedia: Alley Cat (song)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley_Cat_(song))).

## Cuteness is a recipe, and CGA belongs in the accents

The craft guidance agrees on a repeatable formula. Cute characters have a **big head on a short body, a large forehead and small jaw, large eyes with big pupils, and stubby limbs with no visible joints**. Their outlines are soft and round, with no sharp angles, and a slight waddle and helplessness add to the effect ([Packt](https://www.packtpub.com/en-in/learning/how-to-tutorials/planning-characters-look)). Less detail also helps, because "too many details only disrupts and dilutes the overall picture" ([Envato Tuts+](https://design.tutsplus.com/articles/the-elements-of-cute-character-design--vector-3533)). These are craft heuristics, not research. The following application to the cat is a proposal. Make the head about 40–50% of sprite height, with large eyes with one highlight each and ears with rounded tips. Draw the "black" cat in very dark blue or purple so its face can be shaded, and give it a light rim or outline so it stays visible against night backgrounds. Enemies should look grumpy rather than menacing: a round bulldog, and a broom with no visible owner. **Little Kitty, Big City** shows that last idea works. Its humans have no faces because, its director says, cats treat people more like objects ([GoNintendo](https://gonintendo.com/contents/22933-little-kitty-big-city-dev-explains-the-game-s-faceless-humans)). Its "cute and lively animations" carry its charm, and they were made by a single animator ([MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2023/06/10/little-kitty-big-city-preview/); [Little Kitty Big City](https://www.littlekittybigcity.com/about-us)). Other reference games are useful as suggestions, though no sources about their art direction were retrieved: Kirby for round silhouettes, Neko Atsume for cat idle poses, Cat Quest for a storybook UI, and Pac-Man Championship Edition for neon-on-black arcade energy.

### Game feel matters more than graphics

The best documented reference is **Celeste**. It renders at **320×180** and uses a published set of forgiveness techniques: coyote time, jump buffering, halved gravity at the top of a held jump, corner correction, and wall jumps that work from 2 px away ([Maddy Thorson, "Celeste & Forgiveness"](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/index.html)). The original's heavy momentum jumps are the main thing to modernise, and these techniques make momentum feel generous instead of punishing. On top of that comes the "juice" layer from Jonasson and Purho's "Juice it or lose it" (GDC Europe 2012) and Nijman's "The Art of Screenshake": tweening, squash and stretch, quickly decaying camera shake, and hit-stop pauses that make impacts land ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-is-your-game-juicy-enough-); [infovore.org](https://infovore.org/?p=5275); [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/learn/game-feel/1)). The original's set pieces suggest specific uses. These numbers are suggestions, not sourced values: washing lines that sag under the cat, a spring on the fence top, a "pop" when a window opens, about 50–80 ms of hit-stop when a mouse is caught, and cartoon stars when the cat is hit by a shoe.

### Resolution and the CGA homage

There are two ways to set the base resolution, and the research notes disagree. **320×180 (16:9)** scales exactly 6× to 1080p, matches Celeste, and keeps the original's 320-pixel width ([Shweep devlog](https://shweep.itch.io/the-machine-that-breathes/devlog/114161/resolving-resolutions)). **320×200** maps 1:1 to the original's CGA mode 4 layout. A detail that is easy to miss affects both choices: the original's pixels were **about 1.2 times taller than wide** on a 4:3 monitor ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). Copying the layouts pixel for pixel onto square pixels at either resolution will make them look squat, so they need redrawing in any case. 640×360 (3× to 1080p) is the option for more detail and room for UI ([Shweep devlog](https://shweep.itch.io/the-machine-that-breathes/devlog/114161/resolving-resolutions)).

For the homage, the iconic palette is CGA palette 1 with high intensity: **black, light cyan, light magenta and white** ([ModdingWiki](https://moddingwiki.shikadi.net/wiki/CGA_Palette)). The original used it for the title, the alley and Rooms 1, 2, 3 and 6. The cheese room, the birdcage and the love scene used **palette 0 (green/red/brown)** ([vibe-alleycat](https://github.com/beyondbeneath/vibe-alleycat)). So a strictly "cyan/magenta" homage covers only about half the game. Four colours are too few for cute shading or for accessibility. The better use is as brand and accent colours (UI frames, title screen, neon window signs, eye glints) inside a larger palette of 16–32 colours, plus an unlockable full-screen **"CGA mode"** shader that reduces the game to four colours. Exact hex values need checking before they are fixed: DOSBox configs use #00FFFF/#FF00FF ([Vogons](https://www.vogons.org/viewtopic.php?p=584316)), but the commonly quoted hardware values are #55FFFF/#FF55FF, which was not verified this session.

Accessibility shapes these colour choices. The Xbox Accessibility Guidelines say no cue should depend on colour alone ([XAG 103](https://devdocs.xbox.com/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/103)), and **5–8% of men** have some colour-vision deficiency ([IGDA GA-SIG](https://igda-gasig.org/?p=3406)). Enterable windows should therefore get a sparkle or arrow and not only a colour change. The game should also offer toggles to reduce screen shake and flashing.

### A solo-friendly pipeline

The default pipeline is to draw and animate in **Aseprite** ($19.99, source-available under an EULA since v1.1.8), export sprite sheets with tagged JSON, and load them as atlases ([Aseprite FAQ](https://aseprite.org/faq/)). Free alternatives are LibreSprite and Pixelorama, though their licences were not confirmed. Spine ($69 Essential / $369 Pro, one-time purchase) is only worth buying if the art goes hi-res and skeletal ([Esoteric Software](https://esotericsoftware.com/spine-purchase)). Kenney's more than 30,000 **CC0** assets work well as placeholders ([Defold asset page](https://defold.com/assets/kenney)). AI art has two concrete drawbacks. First, the US Copyright Office's January 2025 report says prompts alone are not enough for authorship, so unedited output has weak protection ([Clyde & Co](https://www.clydeco.com/insights/2025/07/ai-generated-content-in-gaming)). Second, Steam requires AI-content disclosure ([Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/ai-game-assets-copyright-steam-disclosure-2026/)). AI output also struggles with consistency between animation frames and with staying on a true pixel grid.

## Kawaii-pop chiptune with layered stems and jsfxr blips

The sound should be simple, bright and easy to sing. Kirby is the model to follow: Jun Ishikawa "intentionally wrote simple melodies and chords" because the Game Boy speaker made complex chords sound bad ([Wikipedia: Jun Ishikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jun_Ishikawa_(composer))). Phone and laptop speakers have the same limitation. Pac-Man CE DX's "fast paced, techno feel" is the model for rising tension ([Wikipedia: PMCE DX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pac-Man_Championship_Edition_DX)). The following direction is a proposal from the research synthesis, not a sourced finding. A square or pulse lead and arpeggios nod to the PC speaker, and they sit over electric piano, funky bass, tight drums and glockenspiel. Suggested tempos are about 120–135 BPM with swing for the alley, 140–160 BPM for the rooms, and a 6/8 waltz at 90–100 BPM for the love scene, all in bright major keys. Each room gets its own instrument colour: marimba bubbles for the fishbowl, pizzicato for the cheese room, a sneaky bassoon for the spider. A single-voice square-wave "PC speaker" version of the main motif can link the title screen to 1984 without copying any 1984 melody. Mendelssohn's Wedding March is a natural public-domain quote for the love scene and has been used in DOS-era games before ([VGMPF](https://www.vgmpf.com/Wiki/index.php?title=Wedding_March)). It must be self-arranged, because specific recordings are not public domain.

The adaptive music design suits the structure. **Horizontal resequencing** gives each scene its own cue. **Vertical layering** of 2–4 time-aligned stems lets a "danger" layer fade in when the dog appears. **Quantised stingers** play on catches and clears, and the music is briefly ducked by about 6–10 dB under them. Sample-accurate sync between stems requires decoded `AudioBuffer`s, not `<audio>` elements. MDN says buffers give "more precise manipulation", and recommends scheduling fades with `AudioParam` methods instead of setting `.value` directly ([MDN: Web Audio best practices](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API/Best_practices)). For the timer, there are two approaches. Raising `playbackRate` also raises the pitch, which suits a cute game. Pre-rendered "hurry" versions at +10–15% tempo, switched at a bar line, keep the pitch unchanged.

The game needs about 30–40 sound effects. **jsfxr** generates the blips (jump, pickup, hit) and allows "unrestricted commercial use" ([sfxr.me](https://sfxr.me/)). For more natural sounds, layer a recorded **CC0** meow, bark or splash under a synth attack. Good sources are Kenney (CC0, no attribution needed: [Kenney support](https://kenney.nl/support)), Freesound's CC0 tier, and the Sonniss GDC bundles, which are royalty-free with no attribution and may not be used for AI training ([Freesound FAQ](https://freesound.org/help/faq/); [GameFromScratch](https://gamefromscratch.com/sonniss-27-5gb-sound-effect-giveaway-at-gdc-2024/)). Avoid CC-BY-NC sounds in anything that makes money. Varying pitch by about ±5% and rotating 2–4 versions of each sound prevents repetition fatigue. The original Atari game's signature sounds were barks, meows, the cat-and-dog fight and kisses ([AtariAge forums](https://atariage.com/forums/tags/synth/)), and they are worth keeping in spirit. AI music is changing quickly. WMG and UMG settled with Udio in late 2025, WMG settled with Suno, and licensed models were announced for 2026 ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/warner-music-settles-udio-lawsuit-strikes-licensing-deal-with-ai-platform); [MarketScreener/Reuters](https://uk.marketscreener.com/news/warner-music-group-settles-copyright-case-with-suno-for-licensed-ai-music-ce7d5ed2db8bff22)). Commercial rights are now described as *granted* rights rather than ownership ([Dubspot](https://blog.dubspot.com/ai-music-licensing-explained-2026)). Their status as of October 2026 has not been confirmed.

The browser imposes some practical rules. An `AudioContext` created before a user gesture starts in the "suspended" state ([Chrome autoplay policy](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/autoplay)), so a "Tap to start" title screen is required anyway, and assets can be decoded behind it. Ship **Opus with an AAC/M4A fallback**. iOS Safari is listed as fully supporting Opus only from 18.4 ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/opus)), and earlier WebKit versions had `decodeAudioData` bugs ([WebKit bug 226922](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=226922)). On iOS, Web Audio is muted by the silent switch unless `navigator.audioSession.type = "playback"` is set ([WebKit bug 237322](https://auto-bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=237322)). Mix to about **−16 LUFS integrated with a −1 dBTP true-peak ceiling**, which is the mobile reference level. The console standard of −24 LUFS is too quiet for laptops and phones ([IESD Mix Ref Levels](https://www.audiogang.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/IESD-Mix-Ref-Levels-v03.02.pdf); [Audiokinetic](https://blog.audiokinetic.com/loudness-processing-best-practices-series-chapter1-loudness-measurement-part2)).

## Phaser 4 with Vite covers this game without extra plumbing

**Phaser 4.2.1** (MIT; stable since 4.0.0 on 2026-04-10) is the only engine checked that ships scenes, fixed-step Arcade physics, tweens, particles, Tiled tilemaps, audio and input together, with first-party TypeScript types ([Phaser releases](https://github.com/phaserjs/phaser/releases); [Phaser README](https://github.com/phaserjs/phaser); [Phaser API docs](https://docs.phaser.io/api-documentation/typedef/types-physics-arcade)). Those are exactly the parts an Alley Cat game needs. It has two costs. Its bundle is the largest at about 356 KB gzip, which is still smaller than the audio, and its WebGL renderer was "rebuilt from the ground up", so v3 tutorials and plugins do not always apply ([Bundlephobia](https://bundlephobia.com/package/phaser@4.2.1); [v4.0.0 notes](https://github.com/phaserjs/phaser/releases/tag/v4.0.0)). None of the features removed in v4 (Mesh, BitmapMask, the Spine plugin) are needed for this game. Phaser 4's `pixelArt` and Scale Manager settings were not checked against the v4 docs. Among the alternatives, **LittleJS** (v1.28.0, about 135 KB gzip) is tiny and very active and includes arcade physics, Tiled JSON loading and an on-screen gamepad, but its ecosystem is smaller ([LittleJS](https://github.com/KilledByAPixel/LittleJS)). **Excalibur** is written in TypeScript but is still 0.x, with breaking changes expected ([Excalibur](https://github.com/excaliburjs/Excalibur)). **PixiJS v8** only renders, so physics, scenes and audio would have to be built separately. KAPLAY has had no stable release since June 2025, and Godot's web export does not support C# ([Godot 4.7 docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/export/exporting_for_web.html)).

Start the project with `npm create @phaserjs/game@latest` or the `template-vite-ts` template. The template pins Phaser 4.0.0 and Vite 6, and its default scripts send a telemetry ping to `gryzor.co`. Bump the versions and use the `dev-nolog`/`build-nolog` scripts ([template-vite-ts](https://github.com/phaserjs/template-vite-ts/blob/main/package.json)). Current versions are Vite 8.3.5, Vitest 5.0.5, Playwright 1.64.1 and TypeScript 7.0.2 ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/)). Keep the rules (movement, dog AI, scoring, difficulty tables) in pure TypeScript modules with no Phaser imports and unit-test them with Vitest. Use Playwright for smoke tests of the running game. Use **Tiled** (v1.12.2, May 2026) for the few static scene layouts, because Phaser loads its JSON natively; LDtk has had no tagged release since January 2024 ([Tiled releases](https://github.com/mapeditor/tiled/releases); [LDtk releases](https://github.com/deepnight/ldtk/releases)). The build is a static `dist/` folder that deploys to GitHub Pages, Cloudflare Workers static assets, or itch.io. itch.io needs an `index.html` at the zip root and allows at most 1,000 files ([itch.io HTML5 docs](https://itch.io/docs/creators/html5)).

## Conclusion

Most of the remake's risk is in the game's feel and its legal identity, not in the technology. The 1984 design already has a good shape: a hub with five small challenges, a difficulty ramp that unlocks rooms gradually, a final scene that multiplies your score, and hazards that annoy more than they kill. Its weak points are the controls (heavy, imprecise momentum) and its identity (characters, melody and art that belong to someone else). So the work goes into two areas: Celeste-style forgiveness on the momentum jumps, and a cast and soundtrack that are recognisably new. The unverified scoring tables are a smaller concern than they first seem. They matter only if the team wants a faithful "classic mode". An arcade remake can design its own numbers around the original's shape: room bonuses of a few thousand points, a speed bonus that runs out in about a minute, and a love-scene multiplier that grows steeply.

The CGA finding is the most useful surprise for planning. The original switched between two palettes per scene, so a "CGA homage" can be one of two things: a single cyan/magenta brand look, or a mode that is faithful to each room's original palette. Decide this early, because it affects how the main palette is built and how the posterising shader works.
